That's the broadcast call. April 25th. Barclays Center. Fever versus Liberty preseason. Caitlin Clark hadn't played a competitive Fever game since last August. Nine months on the shelf. Three minutes into her first night back. And she does this.

Twenty-seven feet. One dribble back. Rattles in nothing but net.

The deep step-back. Cold blood.

Now if you blinked, you missed how casually she walked into it. Listen to the broadcast crew — they don't even get to "great shot." They just go "oh my." Because what else are you going to say? She caught it on the right wing, took one dribble back, rose up over Bria Hartley, and let it fly from twenty-seven feet. The net never knew what hit it.

This is the Caitlin Clark signature shot. The deep step-back from logo range is to her what the fadeaway is to Kobe, what the side-step is to Harden, what the one-leg pull-up is to Dirk. And tonight we're putting on a clinic — the seven coldest step-back threes Caitlin Clark has ever pulled.

So let's set the table. Step-back threes are not normal basketball. They're a confidence problem disguised as a shot. You're creating space backward, increasing the distance to the rim, all while the defender gets a free run at you. Most professional guards won't even attempt one from twenty-five feet. Clark lives at twenty-seven.

Number seven on our list — the Stewart step-back from last June.

CC sizes up Breanna Stewart from 31 feet. Crossover. Steps back. Launches it over a two-time MVP.

The broadcast call says it best. "Mark, another long three."

Watch Stewart's reaction. All she can do is smile and shake her head. Even great players recognize greatness.

Number six. The Vegas dagger from her rookie year.

Aces up by four with three minutes left. Clark catches at the top of the key. Jewell Loyd switched onto her, hand right in her face. Clark dribbles once, takes one slow step back, and rises up from twenty-eight. Doesn't even land before the net moves.

The Aces called timeout. Becky Hammon put her hands on her head.

Real money.

Number five — and this one's just disrespectful. The Connecticut Sun, last summer.

Clark gets bumped at the top of the arc. The whistle never comes. Most guards would complain. She just resets, dribbles back two feet, and shoots through the contact. Through the body, through the arm, twenty-six feet. Splash.

I mean if you give Caitlyn this much airspace, you can bet your entire life savings that it's going to be pure butter once the rock leaves her fingertips.

Number four. This one is the Steph Curry one.

October 2024. Indiana versus Las Vegas. CC down a point with eleven seconds left. Pulls up, doesn't get a clean look, gathers, jab steps right, then crosses left and steps back behind the line for the game-winner from twenty-nine.

That isn't a step-back. That's a chess move.

Number three — the spin-back from Atlanta.

Clark drives the lane, sees the help defender slide, spins counter-clockwise on her left foot, and somehow ends up six feet behind the line, completely square to the rim. The Atlanta Dream defender turns her head, and by the time she finds Clark again, the ball is in the net.

That move was so disgusting one of the announcers had to laugh. The game call: "Are you serious?"

And then we get to the Liberty game from last October. Three threes in 38 seconds. The third one of those threes — that's number two on our list.

Three Liberty defenders draped on her at the three-point line, the entire scheme designed to take away exactly this shot. CC dribbles back two more feet to thirty-one feet, rises completely vertical, and ties the game.

Mark — another long three.

Gainbridge absolutely lost their minds.

And here's why number one is number one. Because everything we just watched was Clark already in rhythm. Already sweating. Already in the flow of the game. Number one is what she did on April 25th. After nine months of injury rehab. Three minutes into her first game back.

Cold start. Cold gym. Cold legs.

Twenty-seven feet, one dribble back, no warm-up, no rhythm three before it.

The deep step-back is the hardest shot in basketball when you're deep into a game. It's an impossible shot when it's the second time you've touched the ball in nine months.

So here's what makes this one cold. Most players coming back from injury take a layup. Maybe a mid-range. They ease in. They find their wind. Caitlin Clark scored on her first possession with a floater — fine, normal, smart. Two possessions later she pulled up from the logo.

That isn't easing in. That's a statement.

Stephanie White was asked about it after the game. She said Clark looked like herself. That's coach-speak. The actual answer is Clark looked better than herself. The pull-up looked smoother than it did pre-injury. The release was higher. The follow-through was longer.

Nine months off, and the shot got prettier.

Compare this to the great step-back guards in NBA history. Kobe needed a half to get to a deep three. Harden ramps up over two quarters. Lillard can hit one early but it's usually after a big run. Clark hit hers cold, three minutes into a competitive game, in a season that hasn't technically started yet.

So now the WNBA has a problem. Because every team that watched April 25th saw the exact same thing. Caitlin Clark's signature shot didn't just survive nine months of rehab. It evolved.

You can't game-plan the deep step-back. You can't switch onto it. You can't double-team it. The only thing you can do is hope she misses, and on April 25th, she didn't miss the one that mattered.

The Fever beat the title favorites by 18 points. Clark played 17 minutes. The shot you just watched seven times tonight was the second three of her season.

The first three of her season hasn't even been played yet.

The deep step-back is back. The shot that broke college basketball is back. The shot that drew 56,000 fans to a regular-season women's college game is back. The shot that made Stewart smile and shake her head — back.
