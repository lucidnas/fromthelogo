This Caitlin Clark Stepback is COLD

---

Clark from way downtown… and she buries it.

Hold on. Run that back.

That right there is what nine months of waiting looks like. April 25th. Barclays Center. New York Liberty preseason opener. Caitlin Clark hasn't played a real Fever game since last August. The first possession of the night, she scored on an eight-foot floater. Forty-three seconds later, she did this.

Twenty-seven feet. One dribble back. Splash.

The deep step-back. Cold blood.

If you blinked, you missed how casually she walked into it. The Liberty hard-hedged the screen, Sabrina Ionescu was draped on her right hip, Breanna Stewart was running at her from the paint. None of it mattered. Clark just glided one dribble back behind the arc, rose up like she was alone in a gym at 2 a.m., and let it fly.

Nothing but net.

This is the move. This is the Caitlin Clark signature. The deep step-back from the logo region is to her what the fadeaway is to Kobe, what the side-step is to Harden, what the one-leg pull-up is to Dirk. And tonight we're putting on a clinic — the seven coldest step-back threes Caitlin Clark has ever pulled.

So let's set the table. Step-back threes are not normal basketball. They're a confidence problem disguised as a shot. You're creating space backward, increasing the distance, all while the defender gets a free run at you. Most professional guards won't even attempt one from twenty-five feet. Clark lives at twenty-seven.

Number seven on our list — the Stewart step-back from last October.

CC sizes up Brianna Stewart from 31 feet. Hits her with a crossover. Steps back. Launches it over a two-time MVP.

Watch Stewart's reaction. All she can do is smile and shake her head.

Even the great ones recognize.

Number six. The Vegas dagger from her rookie year.

Aces up by four with three minutes left. Clark catches at the top of the key. Jewell Loyd switched onto her, hand right in her face. Clark dribbles once, takes one slow step back, and rises up from twenty-eight. Doesn't even land before the net moves.

The Aces called timeout. Becky Hammon tossed her clipboard.

Real money.

Number five — and this one's just disrespectful. The Connecticut Sun, last summer.

Clark gets bumped at the top of the arc. The whistle never comes. Most guards would complain. She just resets, dribbles back two feet to twenty-six, and shoots through the contact.

I mean if you give Caitlyn this much airspace, you can bet your entire life savings that it's going to be pure butter once the rock leaves her fingertips.

Number four. This one is the Steph Curry one.

October 2024. Indiana versus Las Vegas. CeCe down a point with eleven seconds left. Pulls up, doesn't get a clean look, gathers, jab steps right, then crosses left and steps back behind the line for the game-winner from twenty-nine.

That isn't a step-back. That's a chess move.

Number three — the spin-back from Atlanta.

Clark drives the lane, sees the help defender slide, spins counter-clockwise on her left foot, and somehow ends up six feet behind the line, completely square to the rim. The Atlanta Dream defender turns her head, and by the time she finds Clark again, the ball is in the net.

That move was so disgusting one of the announcers had to laugh. Three threes in 38 seconds. We've all seen the highlight. But the third one of those threes — that's number two on our list.

Liberty defenders draped on her at the three-point line, designed to take away exactly this shot. CC dribbles back two more feet to thirty-one feet, rises completely vertical, and ties the game.

Gainbridge absolutely lost their minds.

And here's why number one is number one. Because everything we just watched was Clark already in rhythm. Already in the flow of the game. Number one is what she did on April 25th. After nine months of injury rehab. Forty-three seconds into her first game back.

Cold start. Cold gym. Cold legs.

Twenty-seven feet, one dribble back, no warm-up, no rhythm three before it.

The deep step-back is the hardest shot in basketball when you're deep into a game. It's an impossible shot when it's the second time you've touched the ball in nine months.

So here's what makes this one cold. Most players coming back from injury take a layup. Maybe a mid-range. They ease in. They find their wind. Caitlin Clark scored on her first possession with a floater — fine, normal, smart. Two possessions later she pulled up from the logo.

That isn't easing in. That's a statement.

Stephanie White was asked about it after the game. She said Clark looked like herself. That's coach-speak. The actual answer is Clark looked better than herself. The pull-up looked smoother than it did pre-injury. The release was higher. The follow-through was longer.

Nine months off, and the shot got prettier.

Compare this to the great step-back guards in NBA history. Kobe needed a half to get to a deep three. Harden ramps up over two quarters. Lillard can hit one early but it's usually after a big run. Clark hit hers cold, on her second touch of a competitive game, in a season that hasn't technically started yet.

So now the WNBA has a problem. Because every team that watched April 25th saw the exact same thing. Caitlin Clark's signature shot didn't just survive nine months of rehab. It evolved.

You can't game-plan the deep step-back. You can't switch onto it. You can't double-team it. The only thing you can do is hope she misses, and tonight she didn't miss.

The Fever beat the title favorites by 18 points. Clark played 17 minutes. Took ten shots. Made two. One of those two was the shot you just watched on a loop.

That's the whole story. That's the whole league's worst nightmare.

The deep step-back is back. The shot that broke college basketball is back. The shot that drew 56,000 fans to a college regular season game is back. The shot that made Stewart smile and shake her head — back.

Run it back one more time.

Twenty-seven feet. One dribble. Net.

Cold blood.
